Case study

Latent class analysis of social media influencer credibility and purchase behavior

Which consumer profiles are more likely to make purchasing decisions based on social media influencer recommendations? A latent class analysis on Bulgarian survey data, validated by Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test.

Role
Lead analyst
Course
SURV 617, UMD
Methods
LCA, BLRT, Logistic regression
Tools
R, poLCA, ggplot2
Date
November 2025
Sample
n = 376, Bulgaria

The question

Brands spend heavily on influencer marketing, but the audience is not uniform. Some consumers act on influencer recommendations; others ignore them entirely. The interesting question is whether those differences fall into clean, identifiable groups, and whether group membership actually predicts behavior.

I led the quantitative analysis for a graduate research project examining this in Bulgaria (n = 376). Source Credibility Theory proposes three dimensions through which audiences evaluate communicators: attractiveness, expertise, and trustworthiness. The analysis applies two methods in sequence:

  1. Latent Class Analysis (LCA) on twelve credibility indicators to identify unobserved consumer profiles.
  2. Logistic regression linking class membership to purchase behavior to test whether the profiles are practically meaningful.
376 Survey respondents, Bulgaria
12 Credibility indicators (3 dimensions)
3 Latent classes identified
p < .001 BLRT, 3-class vs 2-class

Data and preparation

Twelve indicators captured the three Source Credibility dimensions: four items each for attractiveness (Q12_1 through Q12_4), expertise (Q13_1 through Q13_4), and trustworthiness (Q14_1 through Q14_4). The distal outcome was Q17, self-reported purchase frequency on a 5-point scale.

Original 5-point Likert responses were collapsed into three categories to reduce sparsity and improve LCA model stability, a standard move when category counts get thin: 

# Collapse 5-point Likert to 3 categories for LCA stability
# 1 = Disagree (Strongly Disagree + Disagree)
# 2 = Neutral (Neither Agree nor Disagree)
# 3 = Agree (Agree + Strongly Agree)

map_3cat <- c(
  "Strongly disagree"          = 1,
  "Disagree"                   = 1,
  "Neither agree nor disagree" = 2,
  "Agree"                      = 3,
  "Strongly agree"             = 3
)

for (v in vars) {
  if (!is.numeric(df[[v]])) df[[v]] <- unname(map_3cat[as.character(df[[v]])])
  df[[v]] <- as.integer(df[[v]])
}

Descriptive patterns already hinted at the story the LCA would later formalize. Bulgarian respondents were skeptical of influencer credibility overall, with the lowest mean scores on trustworthiness items (means 1.43 to 1.47 on the 1-to-3 scale) and the highest on attractiveness (means 1.96 to 2.37). People find influencers visually appealing, less so knowledgeable, and least of all honest.

Model selection

I fit both 2-class and 3-class LCA models using poLCA with 20 random starts and a 2,000-iteration cap to avoid local maxima, then compared fit on four criteria. The 3-class model won on every one:

Model logLik AIC BIC Entropy
2-class-3,689.497,476.997,669.540.929
3-class-3,477.267,102.517,393.300.939

Fit statistics alone are not enough. Information criteria reward fit but penalize complexity, and they can disagree. A more demanding test is the Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test, which formally evaluates whether K classes fit significantly better than K minus 1. I implemented BLRT manually for poLCA with 200 parametric bootstrap replicates:

BLRT result

Observed likelihood ratio = 424.48 (3-class vs 2-class), p < .001, B = 200. The 3-class solution is preferred.

I labeled the classes using a two-criterion rule: average Pr(Agree) above 0.35 was the threshold for High-Trust, and three or more indicators where Neutral was the dominant response category marked Moderate. The resulting profiles:

Does class membership predict behavior?

Classes that do not translate into behavior are descriptive curiosities. The substantive test is whether membership predicts the distal outcome (purchase). I recoded Q17 into a binary outcome (ever purchased vs never) and fit a logistic regression with class as the predictor and High-Trust as the reference: 

m4 <- glm(Q17_2cat ~ factor(class), data = d, family = "binomial")

Key finding

Predicted probability of ever purchasing based on an influencer recommendation: 70% for High-Trust, 53% for Neutral, 29% for Low-Trust. The gradient is consistent with Source Credibility Theory and statistically significant for both contrasts (p < .01).

What it means in practice

Three takeaways come out of the analysis. First, the Bulgarian sample is skeptical of influencer credibility as a baseline, with trustworthiness the weakest dimension. Second, the population is not uniform: roughly a third of consumers are credibility-positive, a fifth reject influencers wholesale, and the plurality occupies a middle ground that responds to looks but not expertise. Third, those profiles map onto behavior: someone in the High-Trust class is more than twice as likely to act on an influencer recommendation as someone in the Low-Trust class.

The substantive marketing implication is that trustworthiness, not attractiveness, is the lever worth pulling. Attractiveness scored highest across all classes but did the least to separate them; the dimension that actually splits behavior is trust.

Honest limitations

  • The sample was collected under simple random sampling assumptions without survey weights, which limits generalizability to the broader Bulgarian population.
  • I used a two-step approach (modal class assignment, then regression), which treats class membership as known. This ignores classification uncertainty. A bias-corrected approach such as BCH in Mplus would be a methodologically stronger next step.
  • The design is cross-sectional. The credibility-to-purchase relationship is associational, not causal.
